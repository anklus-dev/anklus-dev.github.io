# The Right to Repair: Autonomy, Design, and Digital Sovereignty

The Right to Repair is a fundamental philosophy, social & political movement, and products design methodology. It supposes that consumers, as the future owners of a product, should be expected to have a 'right' to 'repair' that item. This could branch from ensuring adequate access to parts, to designing a product with future user repair as a valued aspect in decisions.

---

## The Three Pillars of a Repairable Ecosystem

To transition from a model of corporate dependency to consumer autonomy, a technology product must satisfy three criteria:

1. **Information Symmetry:** Unrestricted access to schematic diagrams, diagnostic software, and official service manuals.
2. **Component Democracy:** Unrestricted retail access to OEM replacement parts (batteries, screens, logic boards) at fair market prices.
3. **Design-for-Disassembly:** For example, designs that use standard fasteners (like Phillips-head screws) instead of industrial adhesives or proprietary 'security' screws.

---

## The Structural Enemy: Planned Obsolescence

The primary adversary of repair advocacy is the commercial incentive of **[[Consumer Advocacy/Planned Obsolescence|Planned Obsolescence]]**. 

For a product designer to lay out a plan of degradation, they must have an assurance that their master plan won't be usurped by a naughty consumer that actually wants to *fix* that device before it breaks.

When manufacturers intentionally design hardware with soldered-on components or hard-program software blocks that reject third-party repairs (known as "parts pairing"), they succeed in their mission to restrict product lifespans. This artificial cycle of forced upgrading generates devastating global consequences, which I detail more in [[E-Waste]].

## Ethical Evaluation

As a student of computer science and philosophy, I find the Right to Repair movement represents a core moral collision between corporate utility and individual human rights:

### 1. Failure to 'sell' an item
When a company uses software locks to prevent a consumer from fixing hardware they legally purchased, they are holding an unfair continued 'right' over an object that is no longer their own. They are making decisions post-mortem, on the consumer's behalf, despite holding no ownership over their previous property. This, if thought of from a certain perspective, can be interpreted as treating the buyer as a continuous source of subscription revenue (a mere means to an end) rather than a sovereign, rational agent (an end in themselves).

### 2. The Global Utility
It's necessary to weigh the short-term economic profit of proprietary technology companies against the long-term ecological damage of discarded silicon and child-labor-backed extraction of rare earth elements.

This may seem a laughable statement, but it is what many lobbyists and others fighting the Right to Repair will say, if not in the same flowery and concise wording. 

Needless to say, the net global utility of an open, repairable hardware market far outweighs the narrow, localized profits of proprietary monopolies.

## Economic Equality

Knowing what it's like to be unable to replace a valuable device (such as a phone) due to artificially high repair costs helps highlight an often overlooked aspect of the Right to Repair: The economic inequality.

As mentioned in [[Planned Obsolescence]], when a company places barriers in the place of consumer repair, that often, if not always, raises the overall cost of repair on the consumer, sometimes even to the extent that replacing the item entirely is the cheaper option. As a result, those of lower income status are inherently hurt at disproportionate rates, unable to afford artificially steep repair costs (or a replacement), and forced to go without technology such as a phone, which in our modern world is essential for most jobs.

Companies are simply not incentivized to combat this disparity naturally. It is those who are least able to buy their products regardless who are hurt by the unecessary exclusivity, and therefore a silent economically-marginalized group is left to have no reasonable options when it comes to technology.
