# Planned Obsolescence: Engineered Lifecycle Limits in Consumer Tech

Planned obsolescence is the deliberate design or specification of a product with an artificially limited useful life, forcing consumers to replace items prematurely. 

In the tech industry, this is done using two primary fronts: 
- **Hardware barriers** (e.g., using adhesive instead of screws, non-replaceable batteries)
- **Software locks** (e.g., artificial firmware limitations, dropping security updates for older, functional devices).

## The Ethical Tension

This practice produces mutliple violations of numerous ethical systems. I'll attempt to address the most pressing and prominent of them. As a less formal note, planned obsolescence violates a core tenet of colloquially understood ethical business: treating consumers as mere means to a corporate financial end rather than ends in themselves.

* **The Consumer Autonomy Argument:** When a manufacturer restricts the ability to maintain a device, they restrict the consumer's property rights and self-reliance. This is the central argument behind the [[The Right to Repair|Right to Repair]] movement, which attempts to dismantle these engineered barriers.
* **The Utilitarian Critique:** While planned obsolescence is economically efficient for hardware manufacturers, it creates a massive negative externality in the form of electronic waste (e-waste), resulting in a net negative utility for global society.

## Equality Compromisation
When a producer untilizes a tactic such as planned obselescence, this is often done through actions that harm an items [[The Right to Repair|Repairability]]. As mentioned in that file, this causes at first not obvious income-equality issues.

When a company places barriers in the place of consumer repair, that often, if not always, raises the overall cost of repair on the consumer, sometimes even to the extent that replacing the item entirely is the cheaper option. As a result, those of lower income status are inherently hurt at disproportionate rates, unable to afford artificially steep repair costs (or a replacement), and forced to go without technology such as a phone, which in our modern world is essential for most jobs.
